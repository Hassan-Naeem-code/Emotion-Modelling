\begin{abstract}
We estimate continuous valence and arousal (VA) from faces while \emph{quantifying
how much to trust each estimate}. Our primary model is a deep evidential
regressor that yields aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in a single forward
pass; a split conformal layer turns that uncertainty into prediction intervals
with finite-sample coverage guarantees and a principled abstention threshold. On
real AffectNet VA, averaged over three seeds, we compare a deterministic baseline,
heteroscedastic Gaussian negative log-likelihood, evidential regression, and split
conformal prediction across accuracy (CCC, RMSE), calibration (regression ECE,
sharpness), selective prediction (risk--coverage, AURC), and robustness to a
corruption-based distribution shift. The methods are equivalent on accuracy (CCC
$0.657$--$0.692$, within seed noise) and on selective prediction, and separate
sharply on calibration: evidential regression more than halves the calibration
error of Gaussian negative log-likelihood (ECE $0.011$ vs.\ $0.024$) at equal or
tighter intervals, in a single forward pass. Every uncertainty-aware method stays
calibrated under shift ($|\Delta\text{ECE}| \le 0.006$) and separates corrupted
from clean inputs well above chance, whereas the deterministic baseline ---
competitive on raw accuracy --- cannot quantify uncertainty at all, and its
apparent shift-detection score varies from $0.001$ to $0.987$ across seeds. An
on-device browser demonstrator exposes the same calibrated uncertainty and
abstention to end users, with no imagery leaving the machine.
\end{abstract}
