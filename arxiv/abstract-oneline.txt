We estimate continuous valence and arousal from faces, and we ask the model to tell us how far each estimate should be trusted. Our main model is a deep evidential regressor, which gives both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty from a single forward pass. A split conformal layer then turns that uncertainty into prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees, and into a principled threshold for abstaining. We compare four methods on real AffectNet valence-arousal data over three seeds: a deterministic baseline, heteroscedastic Gaussian negative log-likelihood, evidential regression, and split conformal prediction. On accuracy they are hard to tell apart (CCC 0.657 to 0.692, within seed noise), and selective prediction separates them no better. Calibration is where they diverge. Evidential regression more than halves the calibration error of the Gaussian model (ECE 0.011 against 0.024), and its intervals are just as tight or tighter. Under a corruption-based distribution shift, every uncertainty-aware method stays calibrated (|delta ECE| <= 0.006) and flags corrupted inputs well above chance. The deterministic baseline does neither. It matches the others on raw accuracy but yields no usable uncertainty, and its apparent ability to detect shift swings between 0.001 and 0.987 depending on nothing but the training seed. We also release a browser demonstrator that runs on the user's own device, showing the same calibrated uncertainty and the same abstention, with no images leaving the machine.
